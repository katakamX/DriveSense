## 0. Bottom line

On held-out simulator variants both models comfortably beat the majority-class baseline: logistic regression reaches macro-F1 0.922 ± 0.016, the decision tree 0.915 ± 0.028, against a baseline of 0.128 ± 0.007.

**On real UAH telemetry, both models beat the majority-class baseline.** The logistic regression scores 0.520 accuracy against a baseline of 0.214 (macro-F1 0.451); the decision tree scores 0.518 against the same 0.214 (macro-F1 0.393). This is the central result of M8 and it is stated first rather than at the end of section 7, because a reader who takes only one number away should take this one.

Note what that baseline is: the majority class *of the training corpus*, which is the only class an always-guess model could actually know. It is not UAH's own majority class, which covers 65.0% of these windows — see section 7.

Two things follow, and they matter more than the headline simulator scores:

1. **The in-domain winner is also the out-of-domain winner.** The logistic regression leads on held-out simulator variants (macro-F1 0.922 vs 0.915) and leads again on UAH (0.451 vs 0.393). That agreement is worth something, but not much on its own: two folds' worth of margin over the decision tree is well inside the fold-to-fold spread, and the ranking is not the interesting part of this table. The gap between the simulator column and the UAH column is.
2. **The two models fail differently on `HIGH_RISK`, and the difference is the whole argument for the one that ships.** Of 16 `HIGH_RISK` windows in UAH the logistic regression recovers 10 (recall 0.625) at the cost of predicting the class 95 times — precision 0.105. The decision tree recovers 1 (recall 0.062) from 28 predictions. Over-prediction is a tuning problem with a visible cost; silence on the rarest and most consequential class is a failure nothing downstream can detect.

Section 7 sets out the three reasons this comparison is harder than "unseen data" — chiefly the simulator/real domain gap, measured there on the two corpora this run used. None of them make the result better or worse; they bound what it means.

The honest reading: a model trained only on scripted simulator drives now transfers to real telemetry well enough to be measurably better than guessing (0.451 macro-F1 against 0.214 accuracy for the baseline), and is still a long way from usable — `HIGH_RISK` precision of 0.105 is the clearest single statement of that distance. Transfer being non-zero is a result; it is not a release criterion.

**Features: 22 of 26.** The excluded ones, and why:

- **`rapid_accel_per_min`** — Identically 0.0 at every percentile and every maximum across all 1,709 UAH validation windows (1 distinct value), against 2 distinct values and a 2.007/min maximum on the simulator. The 3.0 m/s^2 RAPID_ACCELERATION threshold is simply never crossed by real phone-accelerometer telemetry in this corpus, so the feature carries simulator-only signal: a model leaning on it would score well on simulator folds and lose exactly that much on UAH, for a reason that has nothing to do with driving. The recalibrated drives reach it far less often than the old ones (which hit 36.1/min — M7b finding (a)), but "less often" is not "never", and a column that is a literal constant on the validation corpus cannot be learned from usefully at any simulator frequency.
- **`stop_ratio`** — Constant 0.000 across all 1,709 UAH windows — exactly one distinct value — because UAH-DriveSet is entirely motorway and secondary driving and no window ever drops below the 3 kph stop threshold. The simulator produces 13 distinct values for it across 80 windows. A feature that varies in training and is a constant at validation time can only be learned from and never generalise; it is the same defect as rapid_accel_per_min and strictly worse, since the simulator side is livelier.
- **`lat_accel_time_ratio`** — Zero in 98.4% of UAH windows (18 distinct values) and 86.3% of simulator windows. It asks what fraction of a window exceeds 2.5 m/s^2 of lateral load, which almost no real highway window does. Near-dead on both sides, so it contributes noise rather than signal; the same cornering behaviour is carried by lat_accel_std and lat_accel_max_abs, which are non-zero in every window of both corpora and, on UAH, take 1,709 and 195 distinct values respectively.
- **`harsh_braking_per_min`** — Zero in 99.2% of UAH windows — 6 distinct values, 14 non-zero windows in the whole corpus — against 3 distinct values and far larger magnitudes on the simulator. IMPORTANT, and deliberate: this is an exclusion from the MODEL's inputs only. The labelling RUBRIC still uses it (AGGRESSIVE_HARSH_BRAKING_PER_MIN = 2.0 in ml/pipelines/labeling/rubric.py) and that is not an inconsistency. The rubric is a human-authored decision list read by people, where one brake application in 30 s is meaningful evidence on the handful of windows that have it; the model is a fitted function that would treat the same near-constant column as a free simulator-vs-real discriminator. Same feature name, two different consumers, two different appropriate treatments — recorded here, in metadata.json and in docs/model-card.md so it cannot read as an oversight.

No UAH row appears in any fold, in the final fit, or in any number in sections 2-6 (ADR 0006). Section 7 evaluates against UAH separately, and states what that is and is not evidence of.

**Macro-F1 is the headline, not accuracy.** Accuracy is reported only next to the majority-class baseline it has to beat, because on a corpus where one class is 34% of windows — and on UAH, where one class is 65% — accuracy mostly measures the class balance. Macro-F1 weights all four classes equally, so a model that never predicts `HIGH_RISK` cannot hide behind the other three.

On macro-F1 the logistic regression (0.922 ± 0.016) **beats** the decision tree (0.915 ± 0.028). `ml/README.md` asks for this comparison to be reported either way, so it is reported: on held-out simulator variants the logistic regression is ahead, though by a margin the fold-to-fold standard deviations above do not clearly separate.

**Whichever way that margin falls, it is not the reason for the artefact choice, and neither is the file format.** `model.json`'s format is a coefficient dump (`pipelines.artifact`), which a tree cannot use — a tree would need its own node serialisation. That was the original justification; section 7 supplies a better one. The two models are close in domain and far apart out of it: on UAH the tree recovers 6.2% of `HIGH_RISK` windows against the logistic regression's 62.5%. Shipping the model that degrades *loudly* rather than the one that degrades *silently* is the right call on a corpus this far from its target domain, independent of which scored higher above.

## 5. Rubric as a classifier, against script intent

The rubric cannot be scored against `rubric_label`: it *is* `rubric_label`, so it would score 1.000 by construction and the number would mean nothing. The only non-circular check available is against **script intent** — the class each drive was authored to produce, recoverable from the recording id (`high_risk-c-seed1204` -> `HIGH_RISK`).

Two caveats, both material. First, intent is a *per-recording* label applied to every window inside it — the same granularity objection ADR 0006 raises against UAH's trip-level labels, and it applies here too: the idle windows at the start of an aggressive drive are not aggressive. Intent is not ground truth; it is what the script was aiming at. Second, M7b's standalone 45-window pilot recordings no longer exist as separate artefacts — bulk generation superseded them — so this runs over the full 1135-window simulator corpus, which is the same comparison M7b section 3 reports, not the 45-window pilot table in its section 2.

| scored against script intent | accuracy | majority baseline | macro-F1 | balanced acc. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| rubric (the labeller itself) | 0.622 | 0.127 | **0.639** | 0.686 |
| logistic regression (out-of-fold) | 0.652 | 0.127 | **0.665** | 0.709 |
| decision tree (out-of-fold) | 0.601 | 0.127 | **0.621** | 0.668 |

The model is trained on rubric labels, so it can only recover intent to the extent the rubric does. Where it scores *lower* than the rubric, it has lost information the labeller had; it cannot score meaningfully higher, and if it appears to, that is a reason to look for a leak rather than to celebrate.

**On this run it does appear to**: the logistic regression scores 0.665 against the rubric's 0.639, so the paragraph above is describing the current numbers, not a hypothetical. Two readings, and this report does not choose between them. The benign one: the rubric is a hard-threshold decision list, so a window a hair over one cutoff gets a different label from its neighbour, while a fitted model smooths across that boundary and lands nearer the intent the script was authored for — the model beating its own teacher on a *third* label is possible for the same reason label noise is not fatal. The one that would matter: `recording_id` determines intent, so any feature that encodes which recording a window came from is a leak. The margin is 0.025 macro-F1 over 1135 windows from 13 variants, which is small enough that neither reading is established. Worth a look before anything is built on it.

## 6. The shipped artefact

`ml/artifacts/model.json` is a **multinomial logistic regression** refitted on all 1135 simulator windows with no fold held out, serialised as plain numbers by `pipelines.artifact` (format `drivesense-multinomial-logreg` v1) rather than pickled. The cross-validated numbers above estimate how this model generalises; they are not measurements of this exact fit, and this report does not present them as such.

The artefact carries 22 ordered feature names, a mean and scale per feature, and one coefficient row per class — everything needed to reproduce a prediction with arithmetic a reader can follow: `z = (x - standardiser.mean) / standardiser.scale; scores = coefficients @ z + intercepts; proba = softmax(scores); prediction = classes[argmax(proba)]`. Its round-trip against the fitted scikit-learn pipeline is asserted at training time and in `ml/tests/test_train.py`, so an artefact that disagrees with the model it came from fails the build rather than shipping.

`metadata.json` alongside it records the ordered feature names, the deliberate feature exclusion and its reason, the training date, dataset hash, git SHA, the fold manifest and the full metrics from this run.

### What this number is not

**It is not a clean held-out test, and this report does not claim it is.** Three things stand between this figure and that claim:

1. **The labels are rubric labels, and the rubric's cutoffs were calibrated on this corpus.** ADR 0006 is explicit: ten hand-set thresholds were chosen, once, by a human reading UAH's own feature percentiles. UAH's *labels* never trained or scored a model and no UAH row ever entered the training parquet — that guarantee holds — but the yardstick being measured against was shaped by the thing being measured.
2. **There is a domain gap, not just unseen data.** Measured on the two corpora this run actually used: `speed_mean` p50 84.790 against UAH's 91.490 (max 124.411 against 141.144); `accel_std` p50 0.197 against UAH's 0.229 (max 1.621 against 1.311); `lat_accel_std` p50 0.205 against UAH's 0.267 (max 4.950 against 1.736). This tests generalisation across two differently-shaped distributions, which is a harder and less interpretable question than generalisation to unseen samples of one.
3. **The class balance differs sharply.** `HIGH_RISK` is 14.1% of the training corpus and 0.9% of UAH (16 windows). Per-class figures on that few windows carry confidence intervals wide enough to swallow most conclusions.

### Cross-tab against UAH's own labels (qualitative only)

Not a metric — the taxonomies do not correspond and the granularities differ. It is a smell test: windows from trips UAH itself called `aggressive` should skew towards predicted `AGGRESSIVE`/`HIGH_RISK` more than windows from `normal` trips do. If they do not, something is wrong somewhere upstream.

The smell test half-passes, and the half that fails is the informative half. The **ordering is right**: trips UAH itself called `aggressive` draw an `AGGRESSIVE`-or-`HIGH_RISK` prediction on 65.9% of their windows against 16.0% for `normal` trips and 17.9% for `drowsy` ones. The model has not inverted the problem, and on a corpus it was never trained on that is worth something.

The **floor is what fails**: 16.0% of windows from trips UAH called `normal` are predicted `AGGRESSIVE` or `HIGH_RISK`. A severity ordering that only holds above a floor that high is not usable — it is the same over-prediction the per-class precision figures show from the other side, seen here against an independent set of labels rather than against the rubric's.

`drowsy` has no DriveSense equivalent and is expected to spread across the behaviour classes rather than concentrate — ADR 0006's stated limitation, that a vehicle-telemetry rubric cannot separate drowsiness from aggression as a *cause*, applies unchanged to a model trained on that rubric.
